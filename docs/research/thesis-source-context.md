# Thesis source context for the Research Atlas and Design Studio

This record preserves the architectural question and implemented thesis demonstrations after removal of duplicate personal documents from the project tree. Page numbers refer to the 48-page presentation and 104-page booklet identified by SHA-256 in [the source inventory](../reports/revision-2026-10/source-inventory.md). The original copies remain in the owner's Windows thesis folders. Thesis statements are historical claims, not replacements for the [investigator-corrected acquisition protocol](../protocol/acquisition.md), [rating provenance](../protocol/ratings.md), or the currently approved [v1.2 analysis specification](../specs/analysis-v1.2-draft.md).

## Research idea and contribution

The thesis calls the gap between built-environment models and occupants' changing physiological experience the **static environment problem**. Its question asks whether linked spatial and human metrics can support emotional inference for design tools, extending the kinds of feedback available alongside BIM and wellness frameworks (presentation pp. 2–4, 9–10; booklet pp. 11–15). The proposed contribution has three parts: a structured room/response dataset, an experimental spatial-to-affect model, and interfaces that bring that model into a designer's workflow. It is a research hypothesis and proof of concept, not evidence that the present pilot can predict an unseen room or detect an individual's emotion reliably.

The thesis uses valence (pleasant–unpleasant) and arousal (activated–calm) as the displayed two-dimensional affect plane, drawing on a broader valence–arousal–dominance discussion (presentation p. 7; booklet p. 22). The current revision retains **separate physiology-derived, self-reported and fused positions** on that plane. The owner has chosen physiology as the main research focus, self-report as comparison, and a fused position as the Design Studio prediction target. A Neuro-Score expresses proximity of the predicted fused position to a visitor's chosen emotional target; it is not a direct EEG measurement, health score, probability or general design-quality grade.

## Experiment and measurement provenance

The thesis diagrams distinguish Form (room geometry), Lighting (openings and luminous attributes) and Function (room use) across thirty rendered rooms (presentation pp. 12, 14–15; booklet pp. 36–39). The implementation inventory has 160 original recordings, ten unique subject codes and thirty room IDs. Experiment 1 measured comfort, which cannot be plotted as valence. Experiments 2 and 3 supply valence–arousal ratings but used different elicitation procedures; the twenty rated rooms and 110 rating trials require separate-first reporting. Source room metrics were supplied through Grasshopper/Ladybug/Honeybee and are not calibrated measurements of headset exposure. These distinctions are recorded in the protocol and data card.

The presentation's apparatus diagram shows two forehead channels and a heart channel (pp. 16–20). The later investigator report identifies an Upside Down Labs NPG Lite/Chords setup, approximate right and left forehead placements, and wrist ECG, with unknown reference wiring, physical units and hardware sample rate. Booklet statements about a five-minute seated baseline, precisely synchronized 60-second exposure, 250 Hz acquisition and broad questionnaire batteries (especially pp. 40–46) are **not verified for these source files**. The corrected protocol says there are no rest/neutral-baseline recordings or supplied questionnaire responses beyond the recorded ratings. Counter increments do not establish a sampling frequency.

## Thesis analysis diagrams and their limits

The processing diagrams connect EEG/ECG features, self-report, a fused affect target and spatial features to a model (presentation pp. 25–26, 35–37; booklet pp. 51–57, 66–69). The booklet names frontal alpha/beta and cardiac HRV, describes objective/subjective/fused density fields and shows legacy feature-correlation and importance charts (pp. 52–60). It does not provide a reproducible, validated physiological valence mapping. The booklet variously calls beta/alpha a valence measure and describes HRV as arousal; the approved [v1.2 method](../specs/analysis-v1.2-draft.md) instead uses forehead log-alpha asymmetry as a candidate valence component, EEG alpha suppression and engagement as cortical arousal candidates, and eligible HR in the primary arousal composite. RMSSD remains descriptive when independently eligible. These are constructed hypotheses with unverified physical units, not validated emotion measurements; [CP-Spec](../reports/revision-2026-10/CP-Spec.md) records the exact approved source. Raw and derived values must remain distinguishable.

The retired root `PROJECT_REPORT.md` described a historical linear target formula `target = α × objective + (1−α) × subjective` with proposed α=0.6. Its method defaults were never verified as the corrected source protocol. The historical v1.1 implementation used the same algebra with α=0.5 for complete eligible coordinates and separate within-person descriptive versus training-fold population calibration. The approved v1.2 amendment retains α=0.5 while fixing a new component/cohort definition; neither 0.6 nor a physiology-first narrative automatically determines a weight.

Booklet claims of physiological silence, privacy masking, window-area effects, model convergence over 300 random-forest "epochs", statistical confidence, and confirmed hypothesis validation (pp. 25, 58–69, 92–94) should be presented as thesis arguments or historical graphics, **not current empirical findings**. A random forest averages tree predictions; its fitted trees do not train in epochs or use majority voting for continuous coordinates. The earlier 2026-10-08 v1.1 implementation snapshot reported held-out-room equal-axis MAE 0.4304 versus median-baseline 0.4099. The current approved v1.2 [model card](../model_card.md) reports a different reviewed cohort and a `baseline_only` fitted E3 model with no observed gain over fixed room-held-out baselines. Legacy density fields, spectra, room profiles, lighting plots and feature importance remain valuable chart ideas, but their numbers cannot be copied into the revised atlas without reconstruction from eligible data.

## Original Design Studio and later implementation

Booklet pp. 73–78 describe an interactive Design Studio with four input groups: Geometry (length, width, height), Daylight (daylight factor, illuminance, CCT), Openings (counts and areas), and Space Details (walkable area and type). It shows predicted affect coordinates, an affect map, a Neuro-Score, a room view and an **AI Co-Design** action targeting a numerical Neuro-Score. The presentation illustrates the platform on pp. 38–39. The owner's current Design Studio decision retains forward room input and inverse generation on one page. The emotional target point and requested numeric score are two separate inputs. A requested 65 favors a feasible 64.5 over 95 because generation minimizes absolute score difference, subject to locks, ranges and physical validity.

The preserved Windows `Machine Learning/Predictive Atmospheres_Mark 3.zip` contains an earlier Streamlit `src/ui/design_studio.py`, `src/services/optimization_service.py` and `src/services/prediction_service.py`. That version normalized valence as `(valence + 1)/2` for its 0–1 Neuro-Score and converted a target score back into a target valence. Its optimization is historical behavior, not the accepted current formula. The active `backend/src/pa/scoring/neuro_score.py` instead computes proximity in the signed two-axis square, `1 - distance / sqrt(8)`, clipped to 0–1; the UI expresses it as 0–100. The earlier 2026-10-08 implementation snapshot ranked **highest** proximity and lacked a requested-score, lock and range contract. The current `backend/src/pa/optimize/search.py` ranks absolute distance from the requested score, validates locks/ranges through shared room inputs, and reports requested, achieved and delta values. This remains an experimental search on a baseline-only model, not evidence of design benefit.

Booklet pp. 80–85 and presentation pp. 40–41 describe a **Grasshopper plugin demonstration**: a Python node serializes live Rhino/Grasshopper geometry and luminous attributes to a backend API and displays returned coordinates and score in the canvas. The Windows source includes `Rhino Integration/trial_5 final term 3 plugin.gh`. Booklet pp. 86–89 and presentation pp. 42–43 describe a separate **AI rendering demonstration** using the Rhino camera and an attribute/prediction prompt; the Windows source includes `trial_6 nanobanana.gh`. These were thesis prototypes. The Research Atlas may explain and illustrate them as historical demonstrations, but it does not claim live Rhino integration or promise that generated imagery is an observed room render. Studied-room photos/renders and schematic generated geometry need clear labels.
